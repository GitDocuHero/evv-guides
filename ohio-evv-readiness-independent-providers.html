Ohio EVV Readiness Guide

What October 6 means for your visits and your pay

For independent providers in Ohio DD. What actually changes, how verification works on a normal visit, and what happens to your money when a visit does not verify.

Why this matters more to you than to an agency

When you work independently, nobody checks your EVV before it goes out. There is no billing department behind you catching a missed clock-out before it becomes a denied claim. If a visit does not verify, the denial lands on you, and you usually find out weeks later when a payment comes in short and you have to work backward to figure out which visit it was.

That is already true today. October 6 does not change it. What October 6 does is widen the set of services this applies to, which means fewer situations where a gap goes unnoticed.

What is already the rule

Since October 1, 2025, a claim that requires EVV without a matching EVV record is denied. Not held, not flagged for review. Denied. That covers HPC, PD-HPC, and Residential Respite on the Individual Options, Level One, and SELF waivers.

If you have had payments come back short over the last year without a clear reason, this is the first place to look.

What changes on October 6

Senate Bill 315 makes electronic visit verification a condition of payment for in-home care services across Ohio, extends it to nonemergency medical transportation, and requires that clock-in and clock-out happen at the location where the service is delivered. It also directs Ohio Medicaid to publish a public dashboard of EVV performance.

One provision is good news rather than a new burden: live-in family caregivers are exempt from GPS tracking. If you live with the person you support, that likely applies to you.

You can act on this now. Separately from Senate Bill 315, Ohio Administrative Code rule 5160-32-02 already lets a direct care worker who lives in the same household as the person they support request an exemption from visit logging. There is an EVV Exemption Request form on the Ohio Department of Medicaid site. Accepted proof that you share an address includes a utility bill, a current state ID or driver's licence, a W2, or a tax return.

If your living situation changes, you have five business days to tell both the agency you are affiliated with and evv@medicaid.ohio.gov. SB 315 also directs ODM to set up separate oversight for family caregivers, so more guidance is likely coming.

How verification actually works on a visit

Stripped of jargon, three things have to line up.

The visit has a start and an end

You clock in when you begin and clock out when you finish. Both. A visit with only one of the two does not verify, and that is the single most common reason an independent provider does not get paid for work they genuinely did.

The visit is tied to the right person and the right service

The individual's Medicaid identifier and the service code both have to match what gets billed. If you deliver something other than what is authorized, there is nothing for the claim to match against.

The visit falls inside what was authorized

The ISP has dates. Work delivered outside those dates does not verify, even when the work was needed and you did it well.

The shortest useful list

  1. Clock out every time

    If you fix nothing else, fix this. One forgotten clock-out is one unpaid visit, and nobody else is going to catch it.

  2. Clock in where the service happens

    In the home or in the community, not from the car and not before you walk in. October 6 makes the location requirement explicit.

  3. Check the individual's Medicaid identifier once, carefully

    One wrong digit quietly breaks every visit for that person until somebody notices.

  4. Know your ISP dates

    Know when the authorization runs out before you deliver past it, not after.

  5. Report a broken clock event the same day

    If the app fails or you lose signal, say so immediately. A same-week correction is routine. The same correction after billing is a problem with a paper trail.

  6. If you live with the person you support, find out where the exemption lands

    Watch for the ODM rule rather than assuming either way.

If a payment already came up short

Pull your denials for the last sixty days and sort them by cause using the list above. It is almost never spread evenly. One thing is usually responsible for most of the loss, and once you can see which, it is a change to your routine rather than something you need to buy.

Do not write off a denied visit as unrecoverable without checking why it was denied. Some of these are correctable and re-submittable. The reason code is the whole story, and it is worth reading rather than counting.
